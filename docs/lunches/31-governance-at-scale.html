🏪 Meanwhile at Nusantara Retail · Episode 31

A year after the storefront moved to Azure, Nusantara Retail has seven subscriptions: storefront production and test, the loyalty app, finance's data warehouse, a shared network hub, and two that nobody quite remembers creating — one of them is the Visual Studio subscription Dewi uses for experiments. Next month the company completes the purchase of a twelve-store chain in Sulawesi, which brings two more subscriptions with resources in West Europe. And each of the forty stores still runs a small point-of-sale server in its back office that will never move to the cloud.

The guardrails you built in Lunch 18 protect exactly one subscription. Everything else was configured by hand, a little differently each time. Rizky's old blueprint, which stamped tags and roles onto new subscriptions, stops accepting new assignments at the end of this month.

Ibu Sari puts it plainly: "Next year we will have twenty subscriptions. How do we make every one of them follow the same rules from the minute it exists — without you configuring each one by hand?"

Reading

Minutes 5–30 · 6 scenes
Scene 1 of 6

A hierarchy designed for policy, not for the org chart

In Lunch 04 you met the resource hierarchy: a Microsoft Entra tenant, the tenant root group at the top, management groups, subscriptions, resource groups and resources. Anything you assign at a scope — a role or a policy — flows down to everything beneath it, and costs roll up. At small scale you barely notice the top of that tree. At company scale it becomes your most important design decision, because inheritance is how one rule reaches twenty subscriptions.

A few platform facts shape the design. A management group tree can be six levels deep, not counting the tenant root or the subscription level. Below the root, every management group and subscription has exactly one parent. The tenant root group's ID is the tenant ID, and you can neither move nor delete it. A management group's ID is permanent once created; only its display name can change. By default, any user in the tenant can create management groups, and every new subscription lands directly under the tenant root group — outside all of your carefully designed rules.

The Azure landing zone architecture from the Cloud Adoption Framework gives you a proven starting shape. At the time of writing (October 2026) it recommends these management groups:

Management group What lives there Typical guardrails
Intermediate root (your prefix, for example nusa) Everything the company governs; sits just below the tenant root group The company-wide baseline: allowed regions, required tags, security benchmark
Platform → Security, Management, Connectivity, Identity One dedicated subscription each for SIEM tooling, central logging, the hub network and identity services Rules for shared infrastructure, managed by the platform team
Landing zones → Corp, Online, Local Workload subscriptions: Corp for apps that need the corporate network, Online for internet-facing apps, Local for workloads on Azure Local clusters Workload-agnostic security rules at the parent, archetype-specific rules below
Sandboxes Subscriptions for learning and experiments, isolated from the corporate network A lighter policy set and strict budgets
Decommissioned Cancelled landing zones waiting to be deleted Deny almost everything new

Notice what is missing. There is no "Finance" or "Marketing" management group, because a department change should not force you to rewire policies. There are no "Production" and "Development" groups either: environments are separate subscriptions inside the same management group, so test is governed exactly like production. Regions do not get their own groups unless a data residency or sovereignty rule genuinely differs between them. The guidance is to keep the tree flat — three or four levels — and to group subscriptions by the rules they need, which the landing zone architecture calls workload archetypes.

Two settings complete the picture. In the hierarchy settings, choose a default management group for new subscriptions (Sandboxes is a good candidate), so a Visual Studio subscription like Dewi's starts under the sandbox rules instead of escaping them. And turn on the setting that requires authorization to create management groups, so only the platform team can reshape the tree.

TipNever hang your baseline directly on the tenant root group. An intermediate root gives you a scope you can rename, test against (a second, small "canary" hierarchy is common) and even replace, while the tenant root stays almost empty. It also keeps an acquired company's subscriptions out of your rules until you deliberately move them in.

Scene 2 of 6

Subscriptions as units of scale — and vending them

If management groups are the folders, subscriptions are the units you hand out. Each one is a boundary for billing, for service quotas and limits, for access, and for blast radius: a mistake or a runaway script in the loyalty subscription cannot exhaust the storefront's quota or wander into its resource groups. That is why mature organisations create many smaller subscriptions — usually one per workload and environment — instead of one giant one.

Creating subscriptions by hand does not scale, and every manual step is a chance to forget something. Subscription vending turns a request into a fully governed subscription through automation. A workload team fills in a form or opens a pull request; a pipeline built with Bicep or Terraform (both have ready-made vending modules) does the rest.

Creating subscriptions programmatically requires billing permissions on an Enterprise Agreement, a Microsoft Customer Agreement or a Microsoft Partner Agreement account, so the lab skips this step — extra subscriptions on a personal account are also easy to forget. The important idea is the order: the subscription is placed in the right management group before anyone deploys a workload, so inherited policies are already waiting.

The same "decide the scope once" thinking applies to everything else you assign:

Control Best scope Why
Company-wide policy baseline Intermediate root Reaches every subscription, including future ones
Archetype rules (for example "no public IPs" for Corp) Corp or Online management group Only the workloads that need the rule get it
Platform team access Platform or intermediate root, activated through Privileged Identity Management Broad rights, but only when needed and with an audit trail
Workload team access Their subscription or resource group, granted to Entra groups Least privilege; no inherited surprises
Budgets Subscription for the owning team; management group for an overall view Alerts reach the people who can act

For RBAC at scale, assign roles to Entra groups, never to individuals, and prefer built-in roles. When you need a custom role, note that its assignable scopes can include at most one management group, and roles with data actions cannot be assigned at management-group scope. Make privileged roles eligible rather than permanent with Privileged Identity Management (PIM), which needs Microsoft Entra ID P2 or Entra ID Governance licences — Lunch 22 covers it in depth. For budgets at higher scopes, remember from Lunch 19 that budgets alert but never stop resources, and that management-group cost views are not available for every billing agreement type (check the Cost Management scope documentation for yours).

Scene 3 of 6

Policy-driven governance at management-group scope

In Lunch 18 you assigned three single policies to one resource group. At scale you bundle them into an initiative and assign it once, high in the tree, with parameters that let the same initiative serve different archetypes — Corp might allow only indonesiacentral, while Sandboxes also allow southeastasia. Remember from Lunch 18 that assignments stack: a resource must satisfy every assignment above it, so a lower scope can tighten a rule but never loosen one. Keep the top-level baseline short, and put stricter rules lower down.

Three rules decide whether this works in practice.

Where a definition is saved decides where it can be assigned. A custom definition saved in a subscription can only be assigned inside that subscription. One saved in a management group can be assigned to that group and anything below it. So save shared custom definitions and initiatives at the intermediate root, even if you first assign them lower down. At the time of writing, a single scope holds up to 500 custom policy definitions and 200 initiatives, and an initiative can reference up to 1,000 policies.

Roll out like software. A new assignment with a Deny effect at the intermediate root can block deployments across the whole company within minutes. Start every assignment with enforcement mode DoNotEnforce: compliance is still evaluated and reported, but nothing is denied or changed. Review the results, fix or exempt what you find, then enforce in rings — a test subscription first, then one archetype, then everything. Resource selectors and overrides on an assignment let you stage enforcement, for example region by region, without creating copies.

Remediation needs an identity with the right roles in the right places. Modify and DeployIfNotExists (DINE) assignments act on your behalf through a managed identity, either system-assigned (one per assignment) or user-assigned (created once, granted roles once, reused by many assignments). The roles come from the roleDefinitionIds in the definition. The portal grants them for you; the CLI and infrastructure-as-code deployments must grant them explicitly. Once an assignment is enforced, new and updated resources are fixed automatically — Modify during the request, DINE shortly after the resource is created (about ten minutes later by default). Existing resources, and anything evaluated while the assignment was in DoNotEnforce, need a remediation task, which fixes one Modify or DINE policy at a time, so for an initiative you name the policy's reference ID.

WarningThe classic at-scale remediation failure: a DINE policy assigned at the Landing zones group must send diagnostic logs to log-nusa in the Management subscription. Its identity has roles inside Landing zones, but none on the workspace, so every remediation deployment fails with an authorization error. Grant the identity the roles it needs on every scope it writes to — including scopes outside the assignment.

No baseline fits every case, so you need a documented way to say "not here". A policy exemption marks a scope (from a management group down to a single resource) as exempt from an assignment, or from chosen policies inside an initiative. It shows up in compliance as Exempt, can carry an expiry date and metadata such as a ticket number, and comes in two categories:

Exemption: Waiver Exemption: Mitigated Exclusion (notScopes)
Meaning The risk is known and accepted for now The policy's intent is met another way The scope is simply not evaluated
Nusantara example The loyalty beta may use West Europe for 30 days while a partner integration is tested A VM that policy expects in Azure Backup is already protected by the Sulawesi chain's backup product A lab resource group nobody reports on
Visible in compliance Yes, as Exempt Yes, as Exempt No
Built-in expiry Optional expiresOn Optional expiresOn None

An expired exemption is not deleted; it simply stops applying, and the resource reappears as non-compliant. Creating an exemption needs permission to write exemptions and the exempt action on the assignment (the built-in Resource Policy Contributor role has both), so decide deliberately who may grant exceptions.

Finally, regulatory compliance in Microsoft Defender for Cloud builds on the same machinery. The Microsoft cloud security benchmark is enabled by default, and you can add standards such as ISO/IEC 27001:2022, PCI DSS v4.0.1 or NIST SP 800-53. On Azure these standards are policy initiatives behind the scenes, so the same inheritance rules apply. The dashboard maps technical controls to recommendations; it supports an audit, but it does not certify you. Some standards or features may require a paid Defender plan — check the documentation before promising a report to the auditors.

Scene 4 of 6

Governance as code — and life after Blueprints

Clicking policies into the portal works for ten assignments, not for two hundred. Policy as code treats definitions, initiatives, assignments and exemptions as files in Git: every change is a pull request, reviewed by a second person, deployed by a pipeline whose identity is the only one allowed to write policy resources. Microsoft's guidance suggests a folder per initiative with files such as policyset.json, policyset.definitions.json and policyset.parameters.json, plus assign.<name>.json files and an exemptions.<name>/ folder. Existing definitions and assignments can be exported with the CLI, PowerShell or Resource Graph, so you can start from what already exists. Larger teams often adopt the open-source Enterprise Policy as Code (EPAC) project, or the policy library that ships with the Azure landing zone accelerators.

The Azure landing zone accelerators package all of this: the hierarchy, the platform subscriptions and a curated policy set, deployed through an infrastructure-as-code accelerator (Bicep or Terraform, built on Azure Verified Modules and wired to GitHub or Azure DevOps) or a portal experience for a first try. Most companies start from an accelerator and adjust, rather than designing from a blank page.

That brings us to Rizky's blueprint. Azure Blueprints (preview) is being retired in phases: no new blueprint definitions from July 31, 2026, no new assignments from October 31, 2026, no changes to existing assignments from December 31, 2026, and full retirement on January 31, 2027. The replacement is a pair of tools you met in Lunch 20: deployment stacks, which deploy and track a set of resources at resource group, subscription or management group scope and can protect them with deny settings; and template specs (or Git) to store and version the templates.

Blueprint concept Replacement
Blueprint definition and its versions Bicep or ARM templates in Git, optionally published as template specs with versions
Policy assignment artifact Policy assignment resources in the template — or your policy-as-code repository
Role assignment artifact Role assignment resources in the template, assigned to groups
Resource group and template artifacts Resource groups and modules in the template, deployed at subscription scope
Blueprint assignment A deployment stack, for example az stack mg create targeting a subscription
Blueprint resource locks The stack's deny settings (denyDelete or denyWriteAndDelete), with a few excluded principals

ImportantOn January 31, 2027 the Blueprints service stops. Deployed resources stay, but definitions and assignments you did not export are removed, and blueprint locks stop protecting your resources. Export, convert to Bicep, and redeploy as deployment stacks with deny settings before that date.

Scene 5 of 6

Seeing everything: Resource Graph at scale and Azure Arc

Governance you cannot see is wishful thinking. Azure Resource Graph answers questions across every subscription you can read in one query, using a subset of KQL (Lunch 30). Besides the resources table, resourcecontainers holds management groups, subscriptions and resource groups, and policyresources holds compliance states, assignments and exemptions — so "which resources are non-compliant, by initiative, across the company?" is a single query instead of a loop over subscriptions. You can scope a query to a management group, and the CLI returns the first 100 rows unless you ask for more.

KQL
policyresources
| where type == 'microsoft.policyinsights/policystates'
| where tostring(properties.complianceState) == 'NonCompliant'
| summarize resources = count() by assignment = tostring(properties.policyAssignmentName)

The forty store servers will never move to Azure, but they can still be governed by it. Azure Arc projects machines and clusters that run elsewhere into Azure Resource Manager, so they get a resource ID, live in a resource group, carry tags and inherit RBAC and policy like any other resource.

Azure Arc for How it connects Governance you gain Cost note
Servers (Windows and Linux, on premises or other clouds) The Connected Machine agent; a heartbeat every five minutes, shown as Disconnected after 15–30 minutes of silence Inventory, tags, RBAC, Resource Graph; machine configuration audits settings inside the OS; Update Manager, Defender for Servers, Azure Monitor Agent The inventory and organisation features are free; add-on services are billed (see each pricing page)
Kubernetes clusters (any CNCF-conformant cluster) Cluster agents deployed with the CLI Azure Policy for Kubernetes (built on Gatekeeper), GitOps configuration with Flux, Defender for Containers Inventory and organisation are free; several extensions are billed (check the Azure Arc pricing page)

A machine that stays disconnected for 45 days can become Expired and must be reconnected by an administrator, so monitor agent status as part of operations. Arc is not for Azure VMs — they are already in Azure Resource Manager.

Scene 6 of 6

How it fits together for Nusantara Retail

Here is the plan you take to Ibu Sari. Create the intermediate root nusa with Platform, Landing zones (Corp, Online), Sandboxes and Decommissioned. Move the seven subscriptions in, set Sandboxes as the default management group, and require authorization for hierarchy changes. Put the policy definitions and the initiative in a Git repository with a pipeline, and assign them like this:

Scope Assignment Effects Identity
nusa (intermediate root) Nusantara baseline: allowed locations (indonesiacentral, southeastasia), required costCenter tag, tag inherited from the resource group Deny, Modify System-assigned, with the role the definition names (Contributor) at nusa
nusa Microsoft cloud security benchmark (Defender for Cloud) Audit, AuditIfNotExists None needed
Landing zones Diagnostic settings to log-nusa DeployIfNotExists User-assigned: Monitoring Contributor at Landing zones, Log Analytics Contributor on the workspace
Corp Customer data stays in Jakarta (allowed locations: indonesiacentral only); no public IP addresses on network interfaces Deny None needed

Sandboxes inherit only the baseline, plus a small budget on every sandbox subscription. Everything starts in DoNotEnforce mode. After a week of reading compliance in Resource Graph, you remediate the missing tags, then enforce ring by ring. The loyalty beta gets a Waiver with a 30-day expiry and Ibu Sari's name in the metadata. Before January 31, 2027, Rizky's blueprint becomes a Bicep file deployed as a deployment stack with denyDelete. The forty store servers join through Azure Arc into a dedicated subscription under Corp, so the same baseline, tags and Update Manager reach them. The Sulawesi subscriptions wait outside the intermediate root until their West Europe resources have a migration plan — then they move in.

Pause and thinkThe Sulawesi subscriptions arrive with storage accounts in West Europe. If you moved them under nusa today, with the baseline enforced, what would happen to those existing accounts — and to the next deployment their team tries in West Europe?

Knowledge check

Minutes 45–50
0/5
5 scenario questions. Honest grading.

Answer in your head, reveal, then be honest with yourself.

Q1

Dewi creates a new subscription with her Visual Studio benefit. It appears under the tenant root group and none of the baseline policies apply to it. Which setting would have prevented this?

A default management group in the hierarchy settings (for example Sandboxes). New subscriptions land under the tenant root group unless a default is configured, so without it they start outside every inherited rule.

Did you get it?
Q2

You saved a custom initiative in the storefront production subscription. Assigning it at the Landing zones management group fails. Why, and what is the fix?

A definition can only be assigned at or below the scope where it is saved. Save the initiative at the intermediate root (or at Landing zones) and assign that copy — the definition's location decides where it can be used.

Did you get it?
Q3

A DeployIfNotExists assignment at Landing zones should send diagnostic logs to log-nusa in the Management subscription. Its remediation tasks fail with authorization errors. What is missing?

A role for the assignment's managed identity on the workspace (for example Log Analytics Contributor). The identity only has roles inside the assignment scope, but the deployment writes outside it — every target scope needs a grant.

Did you get it?
Q4

Ibu Sari accepts that the loyalty beta may keep a storage account in West Europe for 30 days while a partner tests an integration. How do you record this so it stays visible and does not become permanent?

Create a policy exemption with category Waiver, scoped to the beta's resource group (or the single resource), with expiresOn set 30 days ahead and metadata naming the approver and ticket. A Waiver means an accepted risk; it remains visible as Exempt, and after expiry the resource shows as non-compliant again.

Did you get it?
Q5

It is February 2027. Rizky's blueprint was never migrated. What happened to the resources it deployed and to the blueprint locks protecting them?

The resources still exist, but the blueprint assignment and its locks are gone, so nothing prevents someone from deleting them. Redeploy the same templates as a deployment stack with deny settings (and keep the templates in Git or template specs) — the replacement Microsoft recommends.

Did you get it?

Key takeaways

Minutes 50–55

Design the management group hierarchy for inheritance of policy and access: an intermediate root with Platform, Landing zones (Corp, Online, Local), Sandboxes and Decommissioned, three or four levels deep, grouped by workload type.

Subscriptions are the unit of scale; subscription vending places each new one in the right management group with networking, group-based RBAC, budgets and tags before any workload arrives.

Save definitions high, assign initiatives at management-group scope with parameters, start in DoNotEnforce, and give Modify and DeployIfNotExists identities roles on every scope they write to.

Exemptions (Waiver or Mitigated) with expiry dates and metadata keep exceptions visible and auditable; exclusions silently remove scopes from the report.

Governance is code: Git, pipelines, deployment stacks and template specs replace Blueprints before January 31, 2027, while Resource Graph and Azure Arc extend visibility and control to every subscription and to servers outside Azure.
