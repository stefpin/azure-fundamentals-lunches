🏪 Meanwhile at Nusantara Retail · Episode 24

Monday, 7:40 a.m. Pak Budi forwards a message from a supplier in Surabaya: overnight, ransomware encrypted every file on their server, including the invoices they exchange with Nusantara Retail. The attackers want payment by Friday.

Ibu Sari stays calm but asks pointed questions. "Our invoices moved to Azure Storage last quarter. If someone steals a password, can they encrypt ours too? Could we get yesterday's versions back?" Finance adds a requirement of its own: the tax office can audit any invoice from the last ten years, and the auditor wants proof that nobody — not even IT — can quietly change one. Dewi, meanwhile, notices two more problems: the storage account answers requests from anywhere on the internet, and the bill shows seven-year-old invoices still sitting in the hot tier.

Today's question: how do you make Nusantara Retail's invoices recoverable, tamper-proof, cheap to keep for ten years and reachable only from its own network?

Scene 1 of 6

Layers of data protection

Lunch 07 showed how redundancy keeps copies of your data on other disks, in other zones or in another region. But redundancy copies everything, mistakes included. When ransomware overwrites an invoice, every copy is overwritten within seconds. Protection against people, scripts and malware needs different features, and they work best in layers.

Blob soft delete keeps deleted blobs for a retention period you choose (1–365 days); until it ends you can undelete them. Container soft delete does the same for whole containers — useful when someone deletes the wrong one with a single click. Blob versioning goes further: every write keeps the old content as a read-only previous version with its own version ID. Overwrite an invoice ten times and you have ten older versions to choose from. To restore one, you copy that version over the blob, and its content becomes current again.

Versioning and soft delete reinforce each other. With versioning on, deleting a blob destroys nothing: the current version simply becomes a previous version. Deleting a previous version needs the stronger Storage Blob Data Owner role, and even then the version only moves into soft delete until the retention period ends. An attacker has to get through several layers, and wait, before your data is really gone.

Point-in-time restore answers "undo everything since 2 a.m.": it rolls block blobs in a container, a set of prefixes or the whole account back to an earlier moment. It needs versioning, change feed and blob soft delete, and its restore window must be at least one day shorter than the soft delete retention. It can't bring back deleted containers (container soft delete does that) or blobs in the archive tier, and it isn't available in accounts with a hierarchical namespace or alongside immutability policies. The change feed it relies on is an ordered, read-only log of every create, update and delete, kept in the $blobchangefeed container. Read it yourself when you need to know which blobs an incident touched.

Immutable storage prevents changes instead of undoing them. It keeps data in a write once, read many (WORM) state: new blobs can be created and read, but protected data can't be modified or deleted while a time-based retention policy (1 to 146,000 days) is active or a legal hold is in place. A policy can cover a whole container (container-level) or individual blob versions (version-level, which needs versioning; an overwrite then creates a new version and the protected one stays untouched). A new policy starts unlocked, which is meant for short tests: it already blocks deletes, but you can still shorten or delete the policy itself. Once you lock it, you can only lengthen the period, and not even a subscription Owner can delete the protected blobs — or the account that holds them — before it ends. Test unlocked, lock deliberately.

Object replication asynchronously copies block blobs from a container in one account to a container in another, often in another region. It needs versioning on both sides and change feed on the source. Unlike geo-redundancy, the copy is a separate account you can read at any time, with its own tier, rules and permissions. New accounts block replication to other Microsoft Entra tenants by default.

Two safety nets sit outside the account. Azure Backup offers operational backup for blobs and a vaulted backup that copies data into a Backup vault, which survives even the deletion of the storage account (Lunch 17). A CanNotDelete resource lock (Lunch 18) stops the account from being deleted by accident — and if it happens anyway, a deleted account can often be recovered within 14 days, as long as nobody has reused its name.

Feature Protects against How you recover Good to know
Blob soft delete Deleted blobs and versions Undelete within 1–365 days Deleted data is billed until the period ends
Container soft delete A deleted container Restore the container Doesn't cover single blobs
Versioning Overwrites and deletes Copy a previous version over the blob Every version is billed; tidy up with lifecycle rules

Scene 2 of 6

Lifecycle management: let rules do the tiering

Invoices follow a predictable life: busy for a month, checked now and then for a quarter, rarely opened after the first year, deleted after ten. Moving millions of them by hand would be a full-time job. A lifecycle management policy does it for you: a JSON document of up to 100 rules that the platform runs once a day.

Each rule has filters — blob types, up to ten name prefixes that start with the container name, or blob index tags — and actions for the current version (baseBlob), previous versions (version) and snapshots. The actions are tierToCool, tierToCold, tierToArchive and delete, each triggered by a condition such as daysAfterModificationGreaterThan, daysAfterCreationGreaterThan or daysAfterLastAccessTimeGreaterThan (which needs last access time tracking). This is the heart of the rule you deploy in the lab:

json
"filters": { "blobTypes": [ "blockBlob" ], "prefixMatch": [ "invoices/" ] },
"actions": {
  "baseBlob": {
    "tierToCool":    { "daysAfterModificationGreaterThan": 30 },
    "tierToCold":    { "daysAfterModificationGreaterThan": 90 },
    "tierToArchive": { "daysAfterModificationGreaterThan": 365, "daysAfterLastTierChangeGreaterThan": 7 },
    "delete":        { "daysAfterModificationGreaterThan": 3650 }
  },
  "version": {
    "tierToCold": { "daysAfterCreationGreaterThan": 30 },
    "delete":     { "daysAfterCreationGreaterThan": 365 }
  }
}

A few details decide whether such a rule saves money or causes surprises:

  • Respect the minimum storage durations. Space the steps at least 30 days apart after cool, 90 after cold and 180 after archive, or you pay early deletion charges. The rule above keeps each invoice in every tier long enough.
  • Include previous versions. Lifecycle management doesn't delete a current version while it still has previous versions or snapshots, so a delete rule for old invoices needs a matching version action.
  • Mind the redundancy. The archive tier isn't available on ZRS, GZRS or RA-GZRS accounts, so tierToArchive can't apply there.
  • Avoid ping-pong. daysAfterLastTierChangeGreaterThan stops a freshly rehydrated invoice from being archived again the next morning. With last access time tracking, enableAutoTierToHotFromCool moves a cool blob back to hot when someone reads it.
  • Be patient. A new or changed policy can take up to 24 hours to act, and immutable blobs are never deleted while their retention is active.

If you'd rather not write rules at all, the smart tier from Lunch 07 moves blobs between hot, cool and cold for you.

TipLifecycle rules are free to run, but every tier change is billed as a write operation. For millions of tiny files, those operations can cost more than the storage they save — model it in the pricing calculator first, or bundle small files into larger archives before they age.

Scene 3 of 6

Locking the doors: network, keys and encryption

By default a storage account's public endpoint accepts connections from any network; your data is safe only because every request must be authorized. Advanced designs add network controls on top.

The storage firewall has three settings for public network access: enabled from all networks, enabled from selected virtual networks and IP addresses, or Disabled. With selected networks you add IP rules for public addresses (up to 400), virtual network rules for subnets that use a service endpoint, resource instance rules for specific Azure resources, and the trusted Microsoft services exception for services such as Azure Backup. IP rules don't work for clients in the same Azure region as the account, because those use private Azure addresses — use virtual network rules or private endpoints for them. A request blocked by the firewall gets 403 AuthorizationFailure, even with valid credentials.

There are two ways to bring a virtual network closer to storage:

Service endpoint Private endpoint
What it does Lets a subnet reach the account's public endpoint over the Azure backbone, identified as that subnet Gives the account a private IP address on a network interface in your subnet
DNS changes None A private DNS zone such as privatelink.blob.core.windows.net
Reachable from peered networks and on-premises No, only from enabled subnets Yes, over peering, VPN or ExpressRoute — once DNS is right
Works when public network access is Disabled No Yes
Granularity Per subnet and service (Microsoft.Storage) Per account and sub-resource (blob, dfs, file, queue, table, web)
Cost Free Hourly charge plus per-GB data processing

A private endpoint only helps if clients find it. Applications keep using the normal name, such as stnusa24abcde.blob.core.windows.net. Public DNS answers with a CNAME to stnusa24abcde.privatelink.blob.core.windows.net. Inside a virtual network that is linked to a private DNS zone with that name, the zone answers with the endpoint's private IP address; everywhere else, the name resolves to the public IP address as before. A DNS zone group on the private endpoint keeps that A record up to date for you.

WarningThe most common private endpoint mistake is forgetting DNS. Without a private DNS zone linked to the client's virtual network (or a matching record on your own DNS servers), clients still resolve the public IP address — and once public access is disabled, they fail with 403 AuthorizationFailure. Check with nslookup from inside the network before you switch public access off.

Identity controls tighten things further. Disallowing Shared Key authorization (--allow-shared-key-access false) stops anyone using the account keys, and with them every service SAS and account SAS; Microsoft Entra ID requests and user delegation SAS keep working. Check your tools first — some older tools and a few Azure Files scenarios still depend on keys. A SAS expiration policy (--sas-expiration-period) sets a recommended maximum lifetime for new SAS tokens, and either logs or blocks tokens that exceed it.

Everything in Azure Storage is encrypted at rest with 256-bit AES, using Microsoft-managed keys by default. With customer-managed keys you keep the key in Azure Key Vault or Managed HSM (soft delete and purge protection required); the account reads it through a managed identity and can follow new key versions automatically. Disable or delete that key and every read and write fails — a deliberate kill switch, and a reason to protect the key vault as carefully as the data. Infrastructure encryption adds a second layer, but only if you enable it when you create the account.

Microsoft Defender for Storage (Lunch 15) watches the data plane: unusual access patterns, suspicious uploads and signs of data exfiltration. Its optional malware scanning checks new blobs as they arrive and is billed per gigabyte scanned, while sensitive data threat detection focuses alerts on accounts holding personal or financial data.

Scene 4 of 6

Special-purpose storage and scale

Data Lake Storage is a storage account with a hierarchical namespace (HNS). Folders become real directories, so renaming or deleting one is a single atomic operation instead of millions of blob copies, and you can set POSIX-like access control lists per directory and file through the dfs endpoint. Analytics engines such as Microsoft Fabric and Azure Databricks expect it. Some blob features, including point-in-time restore and object replication, aren't available with HNS, and switching an existing account to HNS is a one-way upgrade — check the feature support table first.

Two protocols ride on top of HNS. SFTP lets suppliers upload files with a normal SFTP client, using local users with an SSH key or password that are separate from Entra ID; it's billed per hour while enabled, so switch it off when unused. NFS 3.0 for blobs lets Linux and HPC workloads mount a container. NFS requests can't be authorized with keys or Entra ID, so the account must accept traffic only from virtual networks, through service endpoints or private endpoints. (Azure Files offers NFS 4.1 on its SSD tier.)

A static website serves HTML, CSS and images straight from a special $web container on its own web endpoint, with an index document and a custom 404 page — no web server needed. Content in $web is always readable anonymously, even when anonymous access is disallowed elsewhere in the account. For a custom domain with HTTPS, put Azure Front Door in front of it.

Finally, scalability targets describe how much one account handles before throttling: by default up to 5 PiB, and up to 40,000 requests per second in larger regions such as Southeast Asia (20,000 in others), with similar limits on throughput. When an account or one of its partitions is pushed too hard, Azure Storage returns 503 Server Busy or 500 Operation Timeout; clients should retry with exponential back-off. Very busy workloads spread data across several accounts — by default you can create 250 per region per subscription, and up to 1,000 on request.

Scene 5 of 6

Moving data at scale and surviving a region

Lunch 07 introduced AzCopy and Data Box for one-off copies. At scale, the question becomes how to keep moving data and how to fail over safely.

Tool Moves data Best for Watch out for
azcopy sync Online, scripted, incremental Mirroring a folder or container on a schedule --delete-destination true deletes extra files at the target
Azure Storage Mover Online, managed from Azure Migrating SMB or NFS shares, project by project Needs an agent near on-premises sources
Azure Data Box Offline, shipped device Tens to hundreds of terabytes over a slow line Shipping takes days; order early
Azure File Sync Online, continuous, two-way Branch file servers with a local cache (cloud tiering) Not supported with customer-managed failover
Object replication Online, continuous, one-way Readable copies of block blobs in another region Asynchronous: the copy lags behind

Redundancy can change later. Switching between LRS and GRS (or RA-GRS) is a simple update, although adding geo-redundancy costs a one-time copy to the secondary region. Adding zones — LRS to ZRS, or GRS to GZRS — needs a conversion that Microsoft runs in the background without a completion SLA, or a manual copy to a new account. Archived blobs get in the way of both: move or rehydrate them first.

For geo-redundant accounts you can start a failover yourself:

Failover type When to use it Data loss Afterwards
Customer-managed planned failover Both regions are healthy: disaster recovery drills, or problems with a service in the primary None — it waits for replication to catch up The regions swap roles and geo-redundancy keeps working
Customer-managed (unplanned) failover The primary endpoint is unavailable Writes after the Last Sync Time The account is LRS in the new primary; re-enable geo-redundancy, which copies everything again
Microsoft-managed failover A whole region is lost Possible Microsoft decides when; don't build your plan around it

A failover usually completes within an hour, and clients keep the same endpoint names. Before an unplanned failover, check how much you might lose with az storage account show --name <account> --expand geoReplicationStats --query geoReplicationStats.lastSyncTime. Not every account can fail over — Azure File Sync, for example, isn't supported — so check the documentation's list of unsupported features before you depend on it.

Scene 6 of 6

How it fits together for Nusantara Retail

Back to Ibu Sari's questions. This is the design Dewi presents for the invoices, which stay in Indonesia Central:

  • Recoverable: the working account for current invoices (general-purpose v2, ZRS) gets versioning, 14 days of blob and container soft delete, change feed and seven days of point-in-time restore. Monday's ransomware scenario becomes "restore to 2 a.m." instead of "pay by Friday".
  • Tamper-proof: when a tax year closes, its invoices are copied to a second ZRS account, into a container with a locked ten-year time-based retention policy — tested unlocked first, with finance signing off on the period. A separate account is needed anyway, because immutability and point-in-time restore can't share one.
  • Cheap for ten years: lifecycle rules move invoices to cool after 30 days and cold after 90. Because ZRS can't archive, object replication sends finished invoices to an LRS account in Southeast Asia, where a rule archives them — once the compliance team confirms that a copy may leave Indonesia.
  • Private: blob private endpoints in the hub network from Lunch 25, a linked privatelink.blob.core.windows.net zone, public network access Disabled, Shared Key disallowed, and the storefront reading data with a managed identity.
  • Watched: Defender for Storage with malware scanning on the separate SFTP-enabled account where suppliers drop their files, and a vaulted backup of the invoice containers.

Pause and thinkAn attacker steals Dewi's account, which holds the Owner role on the subscription. Which of these protections can they simply switch off or delete — soft delete, versioning, an unlocked policy, a locked policy, the vaulted backup — and which ones still save the 2025 invoices?

In the lab you build the protection layers on a small scale, rescue invoices from a simulated attack and then close the public door. Next, Lunch 25 builds the hub-and-spoke network where private endpoints like this one live.

Knowledge check

Minutes 45–50
0/5
5 scenario questions. Honest grading.

Answer in your head, reveal, then be honest with yourself.

Q1

Overnight, a compromised script overwrote 2,000 invoices in a container that has versioning, change feed, soft delete and point-in-time restore enabled. What is the fastest way back?

Run a point-in-time restore of that container (or prefix) to a moment before the script started. It uses the versions and the change feed to roll every affected block blob back in one operation, instead of you promoting 2,000 previous versions one by one.

Did you get it?
Q2

Finance wants the 2025 invoices undeletable for ten years, even by administrators, but isn't sure the period is right. How do you proceed?

Create a time-based retention policy and test it unlocked, where it can still be shortened or deleted. Lock it only after finance confirms the period, because a locked policy can only be extended — and it then blocks deleting the protected blobs and the account until the period ends.

Did you get it?
Q3

Dewi created a private endpoint for blob storage, but nslookup from a VM in the same virtual network still returns a public IP address. What's missing?

The private DNS zone privatelink.blob.core.windows.net isn't linked to that virtual network (or has no A record for the account, for example because the DNS zone group is missing). Without it, the CNAME to the privatelink name falls through to public DNS.

Did you get it?
Q4

A colleague's lifecycle rule moves invoices to cool after 10 days and to archive after 20 days. What's wrong with it?

It ignores the minimum storage durations. Each blob leaves cool after only 10 of its 30 days, so you pay an early deletion charge on every one. Space the steps at least as far apart as each tier's minimum (30 days for cool, 90 for cold, 180 for archive).

Did you get it?
Q5

The primary region of a GRS account becomes unavailable and you start a customer-managed failover. What should you tell the business to expect?

Writes made after the Last Sync Time may be lost, because replication to the secondary is asynchronous. After failover the account is LRS in the new primary region until you re-enable geo-redundancy, which copies all data again.

Did you get it?

Key takeaways

Minutes 50–55

Redundancy copies mistakes; versioning, soft delete, point-in-time restore and backup let you undo them, and a locked immutability policy stops them from happening at all.

Lifecycle rules tier and delete data automatically — design them around minimum storage durations, and include previous versions.

A private endpoint gives the account a private IP address, but only DNS makes clients use it: link the privatelink.blob.core.windows.net zone, test, then disable public network access.

Disallow Shared Key, cap SAS lifetimes, and use customer-managed keys and Defender for Storage when the data is sensitive.

Plan for scale and disasters: pick the right bulk-transfer tool, know which redundancy changes need a conversion, and check Last Sync Time before an unplanned failover.
