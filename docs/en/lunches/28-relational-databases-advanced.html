🏪 Meanwhile at Nusantara Retail · Episode 28

Since Lunch 11 the orders have lived in Azure SQL Database. No more USB drive, no more shared sa password. Then Ibu Sari reads a news story about a cloud outage that took a whole region offline for an afternoon, and she brings it to the Monday meeting: "If that happens to our region, how long can't we sell — and how many orders do we lose?"

The rest of the table has requests of its own. The customer service team needs to look up orders but should not see full e-mail addresses and phone numbers. Finance must keep month-end copies of the data for years. And on Friday Dewi ran an UPDATE without a WHERE clause on the test database — "only" the test database, this time.

Today you make the orders survive three different disasters: a failed zone, a failed region and a failed human. You also lock the data down from the inside. How much data could Nusantara Retail lose if a whole Azure region failed, and how quickly could it sell again?

Reading

Minutes 5–30 · 6 scenes
Scene 1 of 6

High availability inside one region

Azure SQL Database keeps more than one copy of every database and fails over automatically when hardware, software or the network fails, and during planned maintenance such as patching. You do not configure any of this; your part is retry logic in the application, because a failover shows up as a short disconnect. How the copies are kept depends on the service tier:

  • General Purpose (and the DTU tiers Basic and Standard) uses remote storage. The database engine runs on a stateless compute node, while data and log files sit in Azure Storage, which keeps its own copies. If the node fails, the platform starts the engine on a spare node and attaches the same files.
  • Business Critical (and Premium) uses local storage. A cluster of database engine replicas — a primary and up to three secondaries — each holds a full copy of the data on fast local SSD. A transaction commits only when a quorum of replicas has it, so a failover is quick and loses nothing. One secondary is readable at no extra cost.
  • Hyperscale splits the engine into parts: compute replicas, page servers that each hold a slice of the data, a log service, and long-term storage. You can add zero to four high-availability replicas and up to 30 named replicas, and the database can grow to 128 TB.

Zone redundancy spreads those copies across the region's availability zones (Lunch 03). It is available for General Purpose, Business Critical, Hyperscale and Premium, but not for Basic or Standard. For Hyperscale you must choose it when you create the database. With zone redundancy, a whole zone can fail and the database keeps running after a short failover. Microsoft's business continuity guidance describes this as no data loss and a recovery time typically under 30 seconds.

Tier Where the data lives Built-in readable copy Zone redundancy Best for
Basic / Standard (DTU) remote storage no no small, non-critical databases
General Purpose remote storage no yes most business workloads
Business Critical / Premium local SSD on several replicas yes, one secondary yes low latency, fast failover, heavy reporting
Hyperscale page servers + log service + Azure Storage yes, with HA or named replicas yes, chosen at creation large or fast-growing databases

What does a failover look like from the application's side? Open connections drop, and for a few seconds new ones may fail with a transient error such as 40613, "database not currently available". An app that waits briefly and tries again — with growing pauses between attempts — never notices. An app without retry logic shows customers an error page for every failover and every patch. Most modern data-access libraries include a retry option; turning it on is one of the cheapest reliability improvements you can make.

High availability answers "what if a server or a zone fails?". It does not answer Ibu Sari's question, because all of these copies live in one region.

Scene 2 of 6

Disaster recovery across regions: geo-replication and failover groups

To survive a regional outage you need a copy of the data in another region. Azure SQL Database offers three ways.

Geo-restore is the budget option. If your backups use geo-redundant storage, you can restore the latest replicated backup into any region. Nothing runs in the second region until you need it, but you lose the changes made since that backup was replicated, and the restore itself takes time that grows with the database size. Plan for minutes to hours.

Active geo-replication works per database. You create up to four readable geo-secondaries, usually in other regions. The primary sends its transaction log to them asynchronously: a transaction commits on the primary first and travels to the secondaries a moment later. Failover is manual and per database. After a failover the new primary has a different server name, so every connection string must change.

Failover groups build on geo-replication and fix that connection-string problem. A failover group replicates some or all databases of one logical server to a server in another region, and fails them over together. Generally available failover groups have one secondary server; more secondaries were in preview at the time of writing. Each group gets two listener names that never change:

  • <fog-name>.database.windows.net — the read-write listener, always pointing at the current primary;
  • <fog-name>.secondary.database.windows.net — the read-only listener, pointing at the secondary.

The listeners are DNS aliases with a 30-second time-to-live. After a failover, the names stay the same and their targets swap, so applications reconnect without any configuration change — provided they retry.

Failovers come in two kinds. A planned failover first synchronises the secondary fully, then swaps the roles, so it loses nothing; use it for drills and for moving between regions. A forced failover (--allow-data-loss) swaps immediately, so transactions committed on the primary but not yet shipped are lost. You also choose a failover policy. Customer managed (recommended by Microsoft) means you decide when to fail over. Microsoft managed means Microsoft fails over all such groups in a region during a widespread outage, and only after a grace period of at least one hour.

Now Ibu Sari's numbers. The RPO (how much data you can lose) of asynchronous replication equals the replication lag: the changes the secondary has not received yet. It is usually seconds. You can watch it in the replication_lag_sec column of sys.dm_geo_replication_link_status on the primary. The RTO (how long you are down) is your decision time plus the failover itself; Microsoft's guidance quotes typically less than 60 seconds once a customer-managed failover starts.

Geo-restore Active geo-replication Failover group
Scope one database one database a group of databases
Secondary running all the time no yes, up to four, readable yes, one secondary server (GA)
Connection after failover new database name new server name same listener names
Typical RPO since the last replicated backup replication lag (seconds) replication lag (seconds)
Typical RTO minutes to hours under a minute once started under a minute once started
Extra cost none until used secondary compute and storage secondary compute and storage

Two design choices deserve care. When you create a failover group, the secondary is first seeded with a full copy of each database, which takes longer for large databases. And the secondary should have the same service tier and compute size as the primary. A smaller secondary falls behind under a heavy write load, which raises your RPO, and it becomes the bottleneck the moment it turns into the primary. If the secondary exists purely for disaster recovery, vCore General Purpose and Business Critical databases can use a licence-free standby secondary to reduce cost.

TipRehearse before you need it. A failover drill is a planned failover during a quiet hour: announce it, fail over, check that the app reconnects through the listener and that reports still run, write down how long each step took, then fail back the same way. A team that has done this twice will make the real decision in minutes, not hours.

WarningA failover group copies databases, not the server around them. Server-level firewall rules, the server's Entra admin and server-level auditing must be configured on the secondary server too, or the first failover becomes a second outage. Database-level items — contained users, database-level firewall rules, masking rules — travel with the database.

Region pairs (Lunch 03) do not limit you: geo-replication works between any two regions. That matters for Indonesia Central, which has no paired region. If customer data must stay in Indonesia, check with your compliance team before you place a replica abroad.

Scene 3 of 6

Backups in depth: the undo button

Replication is not a backup. Dewi's UPDATE without a WHERE clause reaches every geo-secondary within seconds, faithfully copied. To undo a mistake, you go back in time with backups.

Azure SQL Database takes full backups weekly, differential backups every 12 or 24 hours, and log backups about every 10 minutes. Point-in-time restore (PITR) rebuilds the database as it was at any moment in the retention window — 7 days by default, configurable from 1 to 35 days (Basic: up to 7) — into a new database on the same server. You compare, copy the rows you need back, or switch the application over. Three details trip people up. First, a new database has no restore points yet: the first full backup starts right after creation and usually finishes within 30 minutes, and PITR becomes possible after the first log backup that follows it. Second, you cannot run PITR on a geo-secondary; restore from the primary. Third, a deleted database can also be restored while it is inside its retention window.

Take Dewi's mistake as a worked example. She ran the bad UPDATE at 16:59. You restore the database as it was at 16:58 into sqldb-orders-restored and compare the two copies. If only a few rows changed, you copy the correct values back with your usual tools while the shop keeps selling. If the damage is widespread, you point the application at the restored copy instead. Either way, the more precisely you know when the mistake happened, the less good data you throw away — another reason to keep auditing on.

Long-term retention (LTR) keeps selected weekly, monthly and yearly full backups for up to 10 years — the answer to finance's month-end request. Backup storage redundancy decides where the backup files live: locally redundant (LRS), zone-redundant (ZRS), geo-redundant (GRS, the default) or geo-zone-redundant (GZRS). Only GRS and GZRS make geo-restore possible. You can change it later, but only future backups use the new setting (it can take up to 48 hours); the free offer supports LRS only, and not every option exists in every region — check yours.

Problem First tool to reach for
Someone deleted or changed rows by mistake point-in-time restore to a new database
Someone dropped the whole database restore the deleted database
One availability zone fails zone redundancy (automatic)
The whole region is unavailable failover group failover (or geo-restore)
An auditor wants month-end data from three years ago long-term retention backup
Scene 4 of 6

Scaling and performance

You can change the number of vCores or the service tier while the database stays online; only a short reconnect happens at the end. Serverless compute (Lunch 11) scales automatically. Elastic pools share capacity between many databases whose peaks happen at different times. Hyperscale grows storage on its own and adds compute quickly, because the data does not have to be copied to new nodes.

Reporting is a classic performance problem: one heavy month-end query slows down checkout for everyone. Read scale-out solves it in Premium, Business Critical and Hyperscale, where it is on by default. Add ApplicationIntent=ReadOnly to the reporting tool's connection string and its queries run on a read-only replica at no extra cost. A failover group's read-only listener does the same across regions.

To find out why a query is slow, start with Query Store, which is on by default in Azure SQL Database. It works like a flight recorder, keeping queries, their execution plans and runtime statistics over time, so you can see when a plan changed for the worse. Automatic tuning acts on that data: FORCE_LAST_GOOD_PLAN is on by default and reverts to the previous plan when a new one regresses. CREATE_INDEX and DROP_INDEX can add and remove indexes for you, but they are off by default.

Measure before you scale. The database's metrics show whether CPU, data I/O or log I/O is the bottleneck. If one badly written query is to blame, more vCores only make the problem more expensive; fixing the query or adding the right index is cheaper and lasts longer.

Scene 5 of 6

Security in layers

Lunch 15 introduced defense in depth. For a database, the layers look like this:

Layer Feature Protects against Does not protect against
Network firewall rules; private endpoints with public access disabled connections from unknown networks a signed-in insider
Identity Microsoft Entra-only authentication, managed identities, least-privilege roles stolen or shared passwords an over-privileged account
Data at rest TDE with a service-managed or customer-managed key stolen disks and backup files anyone allowed to query
Sensitive columns Always Encrypted administrators reading plaintext; data stays encrypted in the engine a compromised client that holds the keys
Query results dynamic data masking casual exposure, such as support staff seeing full e-mails determined users who infer values with clever queries
Rows row-level security users seeing other stores' rows users with broad rights who can change the policy
Detection auditing; Microsoft Defender for SQL unnoticed misuse, SQL injection, vulnerable settings nothing by itself — someone must act on the alerts

A few details matter in practice. Transparent data encryption is on for every new database. With a customer-managed key in Azure Key Vault or Managed HSM you control rotation, and revoking the key makes the database inaccessible — so both servers of a failover group need access to it. Always Encrypted encrypts selected columns in the client driver, so the database engine never sees the plaintext; secure enclaves allow richer queries on encrypted columns.

Dynamic data masking is the opposite trade-off: the data is stored in clear text, and only query results are masked for users without the UNMASK permission. The server admin, the Microsoft Entra admin and members of db_owner always see real values. Masking functions include default, email, random, custom string and datetime; for example ALTER TABLE dbo.Customers ALTER COLUMN Email ADD MASKED WITH (FUNCTION = 'email()');. Row-level security uses a predicate function and a security policy to filter rows per user — store managers in Medan see only Medan's orders.

Auditing writes database events to a storage account, a Log Analytics workspace or Event Hubs; Microsoft recommends enabling it at the server level. Microsoft Defender for SQL adds vulnerability assessment and alerts for threats such as SQL injection and unusual sign-ins, as a paid plan.

Security and disaster recovery meet in one more place. With private endpoints, each server in a failover group needs its own endpoint in a virtual network in its own region, and private DNS that resolves the names correctly in both regions. Lunch 25 shows how private DNS zones work; design them for the failover case from the start.

Scene 6 of 6

PostgreSQL, MySQL and the plan for Nusantara Retail

The open-source engines from Lunch 11 have their own resilience features. Azure Database for PostgreSQL and Azure Database for MySQL flexible servers offer high availability with a standby server in another zone (zone-redundant) or in the same zone, kept in sync synchronously. Failover is automatic and typically takes 60–120 seconds. HA is not available on the Burstable compute tier. For reads and for regional DR, both offer asynchronous read replicas, which you can place in other regions and promote. PostgreSQL allows up to five replicas per server (more with cascading replicas), MySQL up to ten. Azure SQL Managed Instance has no active geo-replication, but it supports failover groups for the whole instance, with the same listener idea.

Back to Nusantara Retail. Here is the plan you take to Ibu Sari:

  • Zone failure: the orders database runs on General Purpose with zone redundancy in its home region. A zone outage costs a short reconnect and no data.
  • Region failure: a failover group with a customer-managed policy replicates to a second region, after the compliance team approves where the DR copy may live. The storefront connects through the read-write listener; store reports use the read-only listener. The team rehearses a planned failover every quarter.
  • Human failure: PITR retention is raised to 14 days, and LTR keeps monthly backups for the period finance requires.
  • Inside the database: Entra-only authentication with a managed identity for the app, a private endpoint (Lunch 25), dynamic data masking on customer e-mail and phone for the support team, row-level security for store managers, server-level auditing to Log Analytics (Lunch 30), and Defender for SQL.

The answer to Ibu Sari's question is honest rather than perfect. In a regional disaster, Nusantara Retail could lose the orders still in flight — the replication lag, usually seconds. It can sell again within minutes of the decision to fail over, and the drill tells everyone exactly who makes that decision.

Pause and thinkDewi suggests the Microsoft-managed failover policy "so Azure handles it for us". What would that mean for Nusantara Retail's recovery time during a regional outage, and who would decide when the shop moves to the other region?

Knowledge check

Minutes 45–50
0/5
5 scenario questions. Honest grading.

Answer in your head, reveal, then be honest with yourself.

Q1

The orders database is in a failover group, but the storefront's connection string still uses sql-nusa-main-ab12c.database.windows.net. After a failover to the secondary region, checkout fails with "the database is read-only". Why, and what is the fix?

The app still connects to the old primary, which is now the read-only secondary. Use the read-write listener <fog-name>.database.windows.net, whose target follows the primary through every failover.

Did you get it?
Q2

Twenty minutes ago someone ran DELETE FROM dbo.Orders without a WHERE clause. Will a failover to the secondary region bring the rows back?

No. Replication copied the delete to the secondary within seconds. Run a point-in-time restore of the primary to a moment just before the delete, into a new database, and copy the rows back.

Did you get it?
Q3

Ibu Sari asks: "With a failover group, can we promise zero data loss if the whole region goes down?" What do you answer?

Not for an unplanned outage. Replication is asynchronous, so a forced failover can lose the transactions not yet replicated — the RPO equals the replication lag, usually seconds. Only planned failovers synchronise first and lose nothing.

Did you get it?
Q4

Customer service should see orders but not customers' full e-mail addresses, without changing the application. Which feature fits, and who will still see the real values?

Dynamic data masking, for example the email() function on the Email column. Admins, db_owner members and anyone granted UNMASK still see real values. To hide data even from administrators, you would need Always Encrypted instead.

Did you get it?
Q5

Month-end reports slow down checkout on a Business Critical database. What is the cheapest way to move the reports off the primary?

Read scale-out. Add ApplicationIntent=ReadOnly to the reporting connection string so its queries run on the built-in readable secondary replica, at no extra cost.

Did you get it?

Key takeaways

Minutes 50–55

High availability is built in; zone redundancy (General Purpose, Business Critical, Hyperscale, Premium) lets a database survive a zone outage without data loss.

Failover groups replicate asynchronously to another region and give you listener names that follow the primary; RPO equals the replication lag, and RTO is your decision time plus the failover.

Replication copies mistakes too — point-in-time restore (1–35 days) and long-term retention (up to 10 years) are the undo button, and you cannot run PITR on a geo-secondary.

Secure a database in layers: network (private endpoints), identity (Entra-only), data (TDE, Always Encrypted, masking, row-level security) and detection (auditing, Defender for SQL).

Scale and tune with vCores, elastic pools, Hyperscale, read scale-out, Query Store and automatic tuning; PostgreSQL and MySQL flexible servers offer zone-redundant HA and read replicas.

Field notes from StefanusSenior Solutions Engineer · 20+ years in enterprise IT
🗒️

A failover group is a decision, not a feature. In design reviews I ask two questions about every failover group. First: who decides to fail over at two in the morning, and who is their deputy? With the customer-managed policy, Azure waits for a human, so that human needs a name, a runbook and the authority to act. Second: which connection strings still contain a server name? I search the app settings for database.windows.net; every hit that is not a listener name will break on the first failover. Then I test the undo button the same way: once a quarter, I ask the team to restore yesterday's database to a new name and time it. A backup you have never restored is only a hope.
