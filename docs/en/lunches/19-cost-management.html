🏪 Meanwhile at Nusantara Retail · Episode 19

The first full Azure invoice lands on the finance team's desk on a Monday morning. It isn't huge, but it is bigger than the spreadsheet Ibu Sari approved, and the line items read like a foreign language: managed disks, IP addresses, bandwidth, App Service. Pak Budi insists the storefront only needs two web servers. Dewi vaguely remembers a test plan from the serverless experiments. Rizky admits he deleted a VM "but maybe not all of it". Nobody can say which costs belong to the storefront, which to the stores, and which to experiments that should have ended weeks ago.

Ibu Sari forwards the invoice to you with three questions. What are we paying for? Who is it for? And how do we make sure next month holds no surprises?

Today's question: how do you understand, predict and control what Azure costs Nusantara Retail?

Scene 1 of 6

Where the money goes

Almost every Azure price is a rate: so much per hour, per gigabyte per month, per million operations or per gigabyte transferred. Behind the scenes, every resource reports its consumption on one or more meters. A virtual machine reports compute hours on the meter for its size and region. Its managed disk reports provisioned capacity on a disk meter. A Standard public IP address reports every hour it exists. Traffic that leaves Azure is metered in gigabytes. The billing system multiplies each quantity by the price in your agreement and adds everything up. Your bill is nothing more mysterious than that sum.

That simple model hides two traps. The first is that many meters charge for what you provision, not what you use. A 128 GiB managed disk costs the same whether it holds the orders database or nothing at all, and an App Service plan bills for its instances even when no app runs on it. The second trap is time: some meters run for as long as a resource exists, so anything you forget keeps costing money quietly, every hour, until somebody deletes it.

These are the cost drivers you can actually influence:

Driver What changes the price Nusantara Retail example Lever you can pull
Resource type, tier and size The service, its SKU or VM size (B-series vs D-series, Standard HDD vs Premium SSD) Storefront VMs sized for the 12.12 sale all year round Right-size; choose the cheapest tier that meets the requirement
Running time Hourly meters run while a resource exists or is allocated Dev VMs running nights and weekends Deallocate, auto-shutdown, schedules, scale in
Provisioned capacity Disks, plans and reserved throughput bill for size, not use A 256 GiB data disk that holds 3 GiB Smaller disks; delete what nobody uses
Region The same SKU can cost a different amount in another region Indonesia Central compared with Southeast Asia Compare in the calculator, after data residency is settled
Data transfer Data into Azure is free; data out to the internet and between regions is charged Shoppers downloading product photos Compress, cache, keep chatty services in one region
Licences Windows Server, SQL Server and some Linux distributions add a software charge The Windows reporting server at head office Azure Hybrid Benefit, dev/test pricing, Linux where it fits
Extras Support plans, Marketplace software, premium features A third-party firewall image from the Marketplace Read the price before you select Create

There is one more thing to understand before you open any cost report: cost data is not real-time. Usage is collected, priced and loaded into Cost Management in batches. For Enterprise Agreement and Microsoft Customer Agreement subscriptions the data typically appears within 8 to 24 hours; for some pay-as-you-go subscriptions it can take up to 72 hours. Estimated charges for the open month refresh several times a day and only become final after the invoice is issued. A brand-new subscription can need up to 48 hours before every Cost Management feature works.

So when Dewi starts a large VM at 09:00 and cost analysis shows nothing at 11:00, that is not a bug. When you need to know what exists right now, ask the inventory — Azure Resource Graph or the activity log — not the bill.

Scene 2 of 6

Estimate before you build

The cheapest cost surprise is the one you find before you deploy. The Azure pricing calculator (azure.microsoft.com/pricing/calculator) works like a shopping cart: you add products to an estimate, then choose the region, tier, size, operating system and expected usage for each one. A virtual machine defaults to 730 hours, which is a full month; change it to 200 hours and the estimate updates instantly. Where a product offers commitment discounts, the calculator lets you switch between pay-as-you-go, reservations and savings plans and shows any upfront cost separately from the monthly cost. You can save several estimates, export one to Excel, share a link with the finance team and change the display currency. If you log in to the calculator itself, it can show the prices from your own agreement instead of retail prices.

Every price in the calculator comes from the Azure Retail Prices API, a public API that needs no sign-in. That makes it handy for scripts and spreadsheets — in the lab you'll use it to compare pay-as-you-go with a one-year reservation from Cloud Shell. Microsoft prices every Azure service in US dollars; other currencies are provided for reference and can move from month to month.

A good estimate for Nusantara Retail has three habits:

  • Baseline and peak separately. Two web servers most of the year, six during the 12.12 sale for two weeks. Averaging them hides both numbers.
  • The quiet lines. Disks, public IPs, data out, backup storage, log ingestion and the support plan often add up to as much as the "main" service.
  • One estimate per option. A VM design and a PaaS design side by side make the trade-off visible to Ibu Sari.

TipOlder training material sends you to the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) Calculator. Microsoft deprecated it at the end of August 2025. To compare a datacenter — or the server room next to the pantry — with Azure, use the business case feature in Azure Migrate (in preview at the time of writing), which works from discovered or imported server data.

Scene 3 of 6

Pay less for the same thing

Once you know what you need, you can change how you pay for it. Pay-as-you-go is the default: you pay the list rate for exactly what you use and commit to nothing. It is ideal for anything new, spiky or short-lived — and the most expensive way to run a server that never stops. Azure offers five ways to pay less:

Option How it works Use it when Watch out for
Reservation Commit for one or three years to a resource type in a region (for VMs, with flexibility within a size family); pay up front or monthly Usage is steady and won't change size, region or service Unused hours are lost; refunds are capped (USD 50,000 per rolling 12 months); reservations bought from February 2027 for services covered by savings plans can't be exchanged
Savings plan for compute Commit to a fixed amount per hour for one or three years; applies automatically across eligible compute (VMs, App Service, Functions Premium, Container Apps, Container Instances, Dedicated Host) in any region Total compute spend is steady, but sizes, regions or services may change Can't be cancelled; unused commitment in an hour is lost
Spot VMs Run on spare capacity at a deep discount; Azure can evict the VM with 30 seconds' notice Batch jobs, test runs, rendering — work that can restart No SLA; B-series sizes aren't supported; capacity can disappear
Azure Hybrid Benefit Bring Windows Server or SQL Server licences with Software Assurance (or eligible subscriptions), or RHEL/SLES subscriptions, and pay only for the infrastructure You already own the licences Licence compliance stays your responsibility
Dev/test pricing Active Visual Studio subscribers get discounted rates in dev/test subscriptions — for example, Windows VMs billed at Linux rates Non-production environments No SLA; never run production there

A reservation suits Nusantara Retail's two storefront VMs once their size has been stable for a few months. A savings plan is the better fit if Dewi plans to move part of the storefront to Container Apps next year, because the commitment follows the spend rather than a specific VM. A newer savings plan for databases works the same way for services such as Azure SQL and Cosmos DB. When several discounts could apply, Azure uses the most specific first: reservations, then savings plans, then pay-as-you-go for whatever is left.

Azure Hybrid Benefit stacks with reservations: the reservation discounts the compute, and the benefit removes the licence charge. Spot and dev/test pricing are about where a workload runs; reservations and savings plans are about how long you promise to pay.

WarningA reservation or savings plan is a billing promise, not a server. If you delete the VM it was meant for, you keep paying the commitment until the term ends. Buy commitments last: after you have removed waste and right-sized, and only for usage you have watched for a few months.

Scene 4 of 6

See and control spending

Microsoft Cost Management is the free, built-in toolset for understanding and governing spend. It works at different scopes — a billing account, a management group, a subscription or a resource group — so the storefront team can see its own resource group while finance sees everything. Most subscription types are supported, including the free account and pay-as-you-go, but a few offers aren't; at the time of writing, Azure for Students is one of them.

The tools you'll use most:

  • Cost analysis shows actual and forecast costs. Smart views break the total down by resource, service, resource group or subscription; custom views let you group and filter by almost anything, including tags, then save, share or download the result. Grouping Nusantara Retail's costs by service is exactly the "decomposed bill" the finance team asked for.
  • Budgets set an amount for a month, quarter or year at a scope and raise alerts when actual or forecast spend crosses a percentage of it. A budget can have up to five thresholds and five email recipients. At subscription and resource-group scope, a budget can also call an action group, which can run automation — for example, a runbook that stops development VMs.
  • Anomaly alerts use your last 60 days of history to flag a day that looks unusual. They work at subscription scope and run about a day and a half after the day they check.
  • Exports deliver cost data to a storage account on a schedule, ready for Power BI or your own reports.

Azure Advisor, which you met in Lunch 16, adds a Cost tab with recommendations: right-size or shut down underutilized VMs, delete empty App Service plans, review disks that aren't attached to anything, and buy reservations or savings plans based on your recent usage. Most of these rules look at days or weeks of history, so Advisor won't flag something you created an hour ago.

ImportantBudgets are alarms, not brakes. When a threshold is crossed, Azure sends an email (normally within an hour of the daily evaluation) and nothing stops. Combined with the 8-to-72-hour data delay, a mistake can run for a day or more before anyone hears about it. Credit-based subscriptions such as the free account have a spending limit; pay-as-you-go subscriptions don't, so budgets plus a cleanup habit are your safety net.

To answer "who is it for?", you need tags. Cost data carries each resource's tags, so a costCenter tag lets cost analysis split the bill per team — that is showback. When finance actually charges each department for its share, it becomes chargeback. Three facts about tags catch people out. Tags are not inherited from the resource group by default. A tag only appears in cost data from the moment it is applied, so last month's costs won't show this week's tags. And it can take up to a day for a new tag to show up. Enforce tags at creation time with Azure Policy (Lunch 18). If your account is an Enterprise Agreement or Microsoft Customer Agreement, Cost Management can also inherit subscription and resource-group tags into the usage records — the resources themselves stay unchanged.

Scene 5 of 6

Find and stop waste

Most Azure waste isn't dramatic. It is small, steady and invisible in a list of resources that all show a green Succeeded status. These are the usual suspects:

Waste Why it still costs money How to find it Fix
Unattached managed disk Disks bill for provisioned size; deleting a VM doesn't delete its disks unless you ask Resource Graph (diskState is Unattached), Advisor Snapshot if unsure, then delete
Unassociated public IP Standard public IPs bill every hour, attached or not Resource Graph (no IP configuration and no NAT gateway) Delete, or re-use it
Empty App Service plan The plan's instances bill even with zero apps (except Free and Shared tiers) Resource Graph (number of sites is 0), Advisor Delete the plan or move apps into it
Stopped but not deallocated VM Shutting down inside the OS keeps the compute allocated and billed VM power state "Stopped" instead of "Stopped (deallocated)" Stop from Azure, or deallocate
Oversized or idle VM You pay for capacity the workload never uses Advisor right-size recommendations, CPU metrics Resize, or schedule shutdown
Forgotten test environments Everything inside keeps billing Cost analysis by resource group, tags such as environment=test Delete the resource group; rebuild from code when needed

Azure Resource Graph — the query service from Lunch 04 — is the fastest way to hunt orphans across every subscription you can read. It uses a subset of the Kusto Query Language (KQL), and unlike cost data it reflects changes within moments. This query lists every unattached disk you can see:

KQL
Resources
| where type =~ 'microsoft.compute/disks'
| where tostring(properties.diskState) =~ 'Unattached'
| project name, resourceGroup, sku = tostring(sku.name), sizeGB = toint(properties.diskSizeGB)

Similar one-line filters find public IPs with no ipConfiguration and no natGateway, and App Service plans whose numberOfSites is zero. Before you delete anything, check with the owner (tags help), look at the activity log for recent use, and take a snapshot of any disk you aren't sure about. Some disks only look abandoned — Site Recovery replicas, for example.

Cleaning up once is satisfying; staying clean is a habit. That habit has a name: FinOps, a practice in which engineering, finance and the business share responsibility for cloud spend. The FinOps Foundation describes it as a loop of three phases that you repeat, not a project that ends.

Scene 6 of 6

How it fits together for Nusantara Retail

Here is the plan you take back to Ibu Sari. First, inform: every resource gets costCenter (storefront, stores or it-shared), environment and owner tags, enforced by the Azure Policy assignments from Lunch 18. Cost analysis, grouped by costCenter and saved as a shared view, becomes the first page of the monthly finance meeting, and the answer to "who is it for?".

Next, optimize. The orphan hunt runs every Friday: unattached disks, unassociated IPs and empty plans go into a short report, owners have a week to claim them, and the rest is deleted. Advisor's cost tab is reviewed monthly. Development VMs get auto-shutdown at 19:00 Jakarta time. Once the storefront's size has been stable for a quarter, the two web servers move to a reservation — or to a savings plan if the Container Apps migration goes ahead. The nightly job that recalculates prices for forty stores is a good Spot candidate, on a size that supports Spot. If Dewi's team has Visual Studio subscriptions, development moves to a dev/test subscription.

Finally, operate. Each resource group gets a monthly budget with alerts at 50, 80 and 100 percent to Ibu Sari and the group's owner. The development budget's 100 percent alert also calls an action group that stops dev VMs. A subscription anomaly alert catches the unexpected spike nobody budgeted for.

Look at what makes all of this easier: being able to delete things without fear. If an environment can be rebuilt from a file in minutes, nobody keeps an idle copy "just in case". That is the subject of the next lunch, infrastructure as code with Bicep. In Lunch 21 you'll meet cost again as one of the five pillars of the Azure Well-Architected Framework, and Lunch 31 applies budgets at management-group scope for the whole company.

Pause and thinkThe storefront runs on two VMs around the clock today, but Dewi wants to move it to Container Apps within six months. Would you buy a three-year reservation for the VMs now, a savings plan for compute, or nothing yet? What would you want to see in cost analysis before you decide?

Knowledge check

Minutes 45–50
0/5
5 scenario questions. Honest grading.

Answer in your head, reveal, then be honest with yourself.

Q1

Rizky deleted a test VM last week, yet the invoice still shows managed-disk charges for it. What happened, and how do you find every resource like it across the subscription?

Deleting the VM left its disk behind in the Unattached state, and managed disks bill for their provisioned size. Query Azure Resource Graph for disks whose diskState is Unattached (Advisor also flags them after a while) — why: Resource Graph sees every subscription in seconds, while the bill only tells you about it a day later.

Did you get it?
Q2

Ibu Sari wants an email when the storefront resource group reaches 80 percent of its monthly amount, and the development VMs stopped automatically at 100 percent. What do you configure?

A monthly budget on each resource group with an actual-cost alert at 80 percent sent to her email, and on the development budget a 100 percent alert that calls an action group running automation that deallocates the VMs — why: a budget on its own only notifies; it never stops a resource.

Did you get it?
Q3

Two storefront VMs of the same size will run 24/7 in Indonesia Central for at least three years. A nightly price recalculation job can be restarted if it is interrupted. Which purchase options fit each?

A reservation (or a savings plan, if sizes or services may change) for the steady web servers, and Spot VMs for the restartable nightly job, on a VM size that supports Spot — why: commitments reward predictable usage, while Spot trades guaranteed capacity for a deep discount.

Did you get it?
Q4

Dewi created a large VM at 09:00. At 11:00 the cost analysis for her resource group still shows nothing. Should she report a bug?

No. Cost data arrives in batches, typically 8 to 24 hours later and up to 72 hours for some pay-as-you-go subscriptions — why: the billing pipeline collects and prices usage before Cost Management shows it, so use Resource Graph or the activity log for real-time inventory.

Did you get it?
Q5

Finance tagged every storefront resource with costCenter=storefront yesterday. Why does last month's cost analysis still show most storefront costs as "untagged"?

Tags only appear in cost data from the moment they are applied; they aren't added to historical usage — why: each usage record carries the tags the resource had when it was recorded, so enforce tags at creation time with Azure Policy.

Key takeaways

Minutes 50–55

Every Azure charge is a meter: quantity times price. Many meters bill for what you provision or for how long a resource exists, so forgotten resources cost money every hour.

Cost data is hours behind (typically 8–24 hours, up to 72 for some pay-as-you-go subscriptions); use Resource Graph for real-time inventory and budgets for early warning.

Estimate before you build with the pricing calculator; the old TCO calculator is gone, so use the Azure Migrate business case for datacenter comparisons.

Remove waste and right-size first, then commit: reservations for stable usage, savings plans for flexible compute, Spot for interruptible work, Hybrid Benefit and dev/test pricing for licences and non-production.

Budgets are alarms, not brakes. Combine tags, cost analysis, budgets with 50/80/100 percent alerts, Advisor and a regular orphan hunt — the FinOps loop of inform, optimize and operate.

Field notes from StefanusSenior Solutions Engineer · 20+ years in enterprise IT
🗒️

Every resource needs an owner and an end date. When I review a subscription, I don't start with the biggest line on the bill; I start with the lines nobody can explain. Big costs usually have someone ready to defend them. The quiet ones — a disk, an IP address, a plan left over from last quarter's proof of concept — belong to nobody, and that is exactly why they survive. My rule: nothing temporary is created without an owner tag and an expiresOn tag, enforced by Azure Policy rather than good intentions. Once a month I sort by that date and send each owner a short list. People rarely defend a resource that carries their own name; they delete it. And when someone says "keep it, just in case", I ask one question: could we rebuild it from code in ten minutes?
