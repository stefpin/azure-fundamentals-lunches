🏪 Meanwhile at Nusantara Retail · Episode 02

The finance team has forwarded you a quote: a bigger server for the pantry room, delivery in six weeks, with a five-year depreciation schedule attached. Ibu Sari added one line on top: "Or should we do this cloud thing everyone talks about? Explain it to me in money and in risk."

Dewi has already made up her mind: "Give me a platform. I don't want to patch servers ever again." Pak Budi is less sure: "If it runs in someone else's datacenter, who fixes it when it breaks? And who gets blamed?" They are asking the same question from opposite sides.

Before you can price anything, you need a shared vocabulary: what exactly you rent, what you still own, and what happens to the bill when traffic triples again.

Today's question: what does moving to the cloud really change, for money, for responsibility, and for the risk that one server takes the whole shop down?

Scene 1 of 6

What cloud computing actually is

Cloud computing is renting computing services over the internet from a provider that owns and runs the datacenters. Those services range from raw servers, storage and networks to databases, analytics, AI and complete applications. You create what you need when you need it, through a portal or an API, and you pay for what you use.

A widely used definition from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) lists five characteristics. They are a good test of whether something is really "cloud" or just someone else's server room:

  • On-demand self-service: you create resources yourself, in minutes, without a purchase order or a phone call.
  • Broad network access: you reach the services over the network, from anywhere, with standard tools.
  • Resource pooling: the provider shares huge pools of hardware among many customers and keeps each customer's resources isolated from the others.
  • Rapid elasticity: capacity grows and shrinks quickly, often automatically.
  • Measured service: usage is metered, so you pay per second, hour, gigabyte or transaction.

The test matters because "virtualised" is not the same as "cloud". Pak Budi could install a hypervisor on a bigger server and run five virtual machines on it. That is useful, but he would still order the hardware, wait for it, and own a fixed amount of capacity. Without self-service, elasticity and metering, it is simply a more efficient server room.

An analogy that works well in Jakarta traffic: owning a car versus using a ride-hailing app. Owning a car means paying up front, plus parking, insurance and servicing, for a vehicle that sits idle most of the day. Ride-hailing means paying per trip while someone else maintains the vehicle, and on a busy day you simply book more rides. Neither is always cheaper. They suit different patterns of use, and that is exactly the decision Nusantara Retail faces.

Scene 2 of 6

Money: CapEx, OpEx and the consumption-based model

Capital expenditure (CapEx) is money spent up front on physical assets: servers, storage, network switches, the UPS, the air conditioning for the server room. You pay once and depreciate the cost over several years. To buy, you must guess future demand, and then you live with that guess.

Operational expenditure (OpEx) is money spent on services as you consume them: electricity, internet links, software subscriptions, and cloud services. It is billed in the period you use it, and it can rise or fall with the business.

The cloud follows a consumption-based model: nothing to buy up front, and a bill that reflects what you actually used. Delete a resource and its charges stop. (Careful with the details: data you keep in storage keeps costing, and a virtual machine that is stopped but not deallocated still bills for compute. Lunch 06 explains the difference.) For Nusantara Retail this brings three practical gains: no capital tied up in hardware that might be the wrong size, the freedom to try something for a day and throw it away, and costs that follow sales. The storefront will cost more in December, when it also earns more.

Compare the two on a single invoice. With the new server, finance pays the full price in the month it arrives and then spreads it over five years of depreciation, whether the server is busy or idle. With Azure, the storefront becomes a line in the monthly operating budget: higher in December, lower in February, and gone the day you delete it.

The purchase price is also only part of the story. The total cost of ownership (TCO) of the pantry server includes electricity, cooling, floor space, the maintenance contract, spare disks, software licences, physical security and the hours Pak Budi spends looking after it. A fair comparison puts all of that next to the Azure bill. For a real migration, Azure Migrate can discover existing servers and build a business case (a preview feature at the time of writing) that compares on-premises and Azure costs.

There is more than one way to pay. AZ-900 calls these cloud pricing models:

Pricing model How you pay Good for Watch out for
Pay-as-you-go Per second, hour, GB or transaction, with no commitment New, spiky or short-lived workloads; labs The highest unit price; forgotten resources keep billing
Reservations and savings plans A one- or three-year commitment in exchange for a lower rate Steady workloads that run around the clock, such as a production database You pay for the commitment even if you use less
Spot capacity Spare capacity at a deep discount that Azure can take back at short notice Interruptible batch jobs and test environments Never for anything customers depend on
Free amounts Nothing, up to a monthly limit Learning, small tools, prototypes Usage above the limit is billed
Per-user subscription (typical for SaaS) A fixed fee per user per month E-mail, office apps, payroll Paying for licences nobody uses

Warning"The cloud is cheaper" is a promise only you can keep. OpEx is not automatically lower: a virtual machine that runs around the clock at three times the size it needs can cost more over five years than the server it replaced. The savings come from using only what you need (right-sizing, switching off, deleting) and from costs you stop carrying: hardware refreshes, spare parts, the room, the power, and Pak Budi's weekends. Lunch 19 goes deep on cost management.

Scene 3 of 6

Public, private, hybrid and multicloud

A cloud model describes where the cloud runs and who owns it.

  • Public cloud: a provider such as Microsoft owns and runs the datacenters and offers services to anyone over the internet. Customers share physical hardware but are isolated from each other. There is no CapEx, scale is huge, and you can start in minutes; in exchange, you have no physical control.
  • Private cloud: cloud-style self-service built for a single organisation, in its own datacenter or a hosted one. You get maximum control and maximum responsibility. You buy and run the hardware, so CapEx returns, and elasticity is limited to what you bought.
  • Hybrid cloud: public cloud and private or on-premises environments, connected so that they work together. You choose where each workload runs. Hybrid is common when some systems cannot move yet, because of regulation, latency or a recent hardware investment.
  • Multicloud: two or more public cloud providers used at the same time, for example because an acquired company runs on another provider or one service fits a need better. It adds overhead: every provider has its own tools, identities, skills and bills.

Azure Arc reduces that overhead. It projects servers, Kubernetes clusters and SQL Server instances that run outside Azure (on-premises, at the edge or in another cloud) into Azure Resource Manager. They then appear next to your Azure resources, and you can tag them, govern them with Azure Policy, control access with Azure RBAC, and monitor and secure them with the same tools. Arc's core control-plane features cost nothing extra; services you add on top, such as Microsoft Defender for Cloud or Azure Monitor, are billed as usual.

Model Who owns the hardware Typical use case At Nusantara Retail
Public The cloud provider New applications, websites, variable demand The storefront in Azure
Private Your organisation, or a host acting for you Strict control requirements, existing datacenter investment Not planned: one server room is not a private cloud
Hybrid Both, connected Gradual migration; systems that must stay on-premises Store systems stay in the stores; the storefront moves to Azure
Multicloud Two or more providers Acquisitions, specialised services, not depending on one vendor A marketing analytics tool that already runs on another provider
Scene 4 of 6

IaaS, PaaS, SaaS and serverless: who does what

Cloud providers offer service types at different levels of the stack. The higher you go, the more the provider does for you, and the less you control.

  • Infrastructure as a service (IaaS): you rent virtual machines, disks and networks. Microsoft runs the datacenter, the physical hosts and the physical network; you manage everything from the operating system up: patching, hardening, runtimes, your application and your data. Most control, most work. Typical uses: lift-and-shift migrations, software that needs a specific operating system setup, test and development machines. Examples: Azure Virtual Machines, managed disks, virtual networks.
  • Platform as a service (PaaS): you bring code and data; Microsoft runs, patches and scales the operating system, runtime and middleware. Typical uses: web apps and APIs, managed databases, teams that want to ship fast. Examples: Azure App Service, Azure SQL Database, Azure Storage.
  • Software as a service (SaaS): a finished application you subscribe to and configure. The vendor runs everything; you manage your users, data and settings. Typical uses: e-mail and collaboration, CRM, HR and payroll. Examples: Microsoft 365, Dynamics 365.

Serverless is the far end of PaaS: you stop thinking about servers at all, even about how many instances run. Code or workflows run when an event happens, scale automatically (down to zero when idle), and you pay per execution. Azure Functions runs code; Azure Logic Apps runs workflows. At Nusantara Retail, a serverless function could send the order confirmation e-mail the moment an order arrives. Lunch 14 builds it.

The shared responsibility model says who looks after each layer in each service type:

Layer On-premises IaaS PaaS SaaS
Your data You You You You
Settings and configuration You You You You
Identities and access You You You You
Devices (laptops, phones) You You You You
Applications You You Shared Microsoft
Network controls You You Shared Microsoft
Operating system You You Microsoft Microsoft
Physical hosts You Microsoft Microsoft Microsoft
Physical network and datacenter You Microsoft Microsoft Microsoft

Read the table from the bottom up: each step from on-premises to SaaS hands more layers to Microsoft. Now read the top four rows. They never move. Whatever the service type, Nusantara Retail decides who can sign in, enforces MFA, classifies customer data, configures each service securely and looks after its own laptops and phones. A provider can run a flawless platform while you still leak data through one weak password.

The "Shared" cells are where most real incidents happen, because each side assumes the other is looking. In PaaS, Microsoft patches the web server software under Dewi's app, but Dewi still writes the code, keeps her libraries up to date, stores connection strings safely and decides whether the app accepts traffic from the whole internet. In IaaS, Microsoft secures the physical network, but the firewall rules in front of each virtual machine are Pak Budi's job. When you choose a service type, write down who owns each shared item before you go live.

Scene 5 of 6

The benefits the cloud promises

AZ-900 uses specific words for the benefits of cloud services. Each one answers a different worry, so learn them as a set.

  • High availability keeps a service running despite failures. Providers promise it in a service-level agreement (SLA): a percentage of uptime per month, with service credits if they miss it. Every service has its own SLA, and many depend on how you configure them; two VMs in different availability zones earn a higher SLA than one VM alone.
  • Scalability is the ability to handle more load by adding resources. Vertical scaling (scaling up or down) means a bigger or smaller VM; it has a ceiling and usually needs a restart. Horizontal scaling (scaling out or in) means more or fewer instances; it needs an application that can run as several copies behind a load balancer, and it has almost no ceiling.
  • Elasticity is scaling that happens automatically, in both directions, as demand changes. Autoscale adds web instances before the 12.12 rush and removes them afterwards, so you pay for the peak only during the peak.
  • Reliability is the ability to recover from failures and keep working. Availability zones, multiple regions and managed backups are the building blocks; Lunches 03, 09 and 17 use them.
  • Predictability comes in two kinds. Performance predictability: autoscaling and load balancing keep response times steady. Cost predictability: the pricing calculator, budgets and Cost Management let you forecast and track spending.
  • Security: Microsoft secures the physical layer and, in PaaS and SaaS, patches the platform for you. Services such as DDoS protection and Microsoft Defender for Cloud add more (Lunch 15). Your share stays yours.
  • Governance: policies and templates keep deployments within company standards and regulations, and audit logs show who changed what: Azure Policy, tags, resource locks and Microsoft Purview (Lunch 18).
  • Manageability has two meanings. Management of the cloud: autoscaling, monitoring, alerts and templates that look after resources for you. Management in the cloud: the portal, the CLI, PowerShell and the APIs from Lunch 01.

An SLA percentage is easier to judge as minutes. In a 30-day month (43,200 minutes):

SLA Allowed downtime per 30-day month
99% 7 hours 12 minutes
99.9% 43 minutes 12 seconds
99.95% 21 minutes 36 seconds
99.99% about 4 minutes 19 seconds

When a request depends on two services in a row, multiply their SLAs: a web tier at 99.95% in front of a database at 99.99% gives about 99.94% for the whole path. Every extra dependency lowers the total.

These words also turn Ibu Sari's question into something you can measure. Today the storefront's availability depends on one server, one power supply and one person's phone being switched on. In the cloud you can choose a target, such as "no more than 22 minutes of unplanned downtime a month", and then design for it with zones, load balancing and managed services. Keep three ideas apart: high availability keeps the service running through small failures, reliability is the overall ability to recover, and disaster recovery (Lunch 17) brings the service back after a large failure such as the loss of a region.

TipA favourite exam trap: scalability means a system can grow; elasticity means it grows and shrinks automatically with demand. Every elastic system is scalable, but not every scalable system is elastic.

Scene 6 of 6

Applying it to Nusantara Retail's single server

Back to the quote on Ibu Sari's desk. You can now lay out three options in the language she asked for:

  1. Buy the bigger server. CapEx up front and a six-week wait. It is still one box, so a failure still stops sales. Its fixed capacity sits idle most of the year and may still be too small on 12.12. Nusantara owns every layer.
  2. Lift and shift to IaaS. Move the storefront to Azure virtual machines. It is quick and familiar, there is no hardware to buy, and two VMs in different availability zones behind a load balancer remove the single point of failure. But Pak Budi still patches operating systems and manages backups.
  3. Move to PaaS. Run the storefront on App Service and its database on Azure SQL Database. Microsoft patches the platform, backups are built in, and autoscale handles 12.12. Dewi may need to change some code and configuration. Nusantara keeps its data, identities and settings.

Whatever you choose, a few questions decide it as much as the price does. Does the team have the skills to run it? How soon must the risk of the pantry server be gone? Where must customer data live? (For Nusantara Retail, that points to the Indonesia Central region.) And who will be on call at 2 a.m., Pak Budi or the platform?

Many companies do both: lift and shift first to get off the old hardware quickly, then modernise to PaaS. The store systems can stay on-premises for now, which makes Nusantara Retail a hybrid company, and Azure Arc can bring them under the same governance later. In Lab 02 you will price options 2 and 3 with the Azure pricing calculator and write a one-page recommendation for Ibu Sari. Lunch 03 then answers the next question: where in the world should all of this run?

Pause and thinkNusantara Retail has moved the storefront to PaaS. Six months later, customer data leaks because a store manager's account without MFA was phished. Under the shared responsibility model, whose failure is it, and which row of the table does it belong to?

The finance team asks: "Is the cloud just a different way of buying servers?" How do you explain the change in spending for the storefront?

It moves spending from CapEx (buy a server up front and depreciate it) to OpEx (pay every month for what you use). Why: the consumption-based model has no upfront purchase, and costs follow actual usage.

The HR team uses an online payroll service in the browser. They never see a server or a line of code. Which service type is this, and who must remove a former employee's account?

During the 12.12 sale the storefront automatically runs six web instances for two days and two for the rest of the month. Which benefit describes this best: scalability or elasticity?

Key takeaways

Minutes 50–55

Cloud computing means renting computing services on demand over the internet: self-service, pooled, elastic and metered.

The consumption-based model moves spending from CapEx to OpEx. Pricing models (pay-as-you-go, reservations and savings plans, spot, free amounts, per-user subscriptions) trade flexibility against price.

Public, private, hybrid and multicloud describe where workloads run and who owns the hardware; Azure Arc brings servers and clusters outside Azure under Azure management.

IaaS, PaaS and SaaS hand more of the stack to the provider, and serverless goes furthest for event-driven code. Your data, settings, identities and devices always stay yours.

High availability, scalability, elasticity, reliability, predictability, security, governance and manageability are benefits you design for. They do not happen just because something runs in the cloud.

Field notes from StefanusSenior Solutions Engineer · 20+ years in enterprise IT
🗒️

An SLA is a refund policy, not an insurance policy. When a design document says "99.99%", I ask two questions. First: 99.99% of what? I list every service a customer request passes through and multiply their SLAs, because a chain of services is weaker than its weakest link. Second: what happens when the provider misses the target? The answer is a service credit, a percentage of that one service's monthly fee, and only if you claim it. It does not cover the sales lost on 12.12, or the customers who never came back. So I read each SLA as a commitment for one component, never as the availability of the whole shop. The business target, such as "no more than 22 minutes of downtime a month", is mine to design for, and to test.
